# Instrument Taxonomy

## Purpose and scope

This taxonomy separates economic function from legal form. It is intended to
prevent the project from calling every useful contract a derivative or treating
financial complexity as an objective in itself.

A cellular-agriculture facility may need capital, a customer commitment, a
guarantee, insurance, or a hedge. Those mechanisms solve different problems.
Some can be combined, but each must be described and evaluated separately.

The categories below are analytical, not legal conclusions. Depending on its
terms and jurisdiction, an arrangement may be regulated as a loan, security,
derivative, commodity interest, insurance contract, collective investment
vehicle, commercial supply agreement, public subsidy, or more than one of
these. Qualified legal, regulatory, tax, accounting, and prudential advice is
required before any real transaction.

## Classification by economic function

| Family | Primary function | Provides cash at inception? | Typical payment basis | Principal bearer of risk | Central diligence question |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| Capital formation | Funds development, construction, working capital, or ownership | Usually | Residual value, interest, principal, rent, or revenue participation | Equity investor, lender, lessor, or funder | Can project cash flows and recoveries support the promised return? |
| Project finance | Organizes capital around a ring-fenced asset and contracted cash flows | Yes | Project cash flow under a contractual waterfall | Sponsors and limited-recourse lenders | Are completion, operations, contracts, reserves, and security sufficient for limited recourse? |
| Demand support | Makes future revenue more certain or brings it forward | Sometimes | Delivery, availability, purchase, or market-price difference | Buyer, sponsor, grant maker, or public authority | Is the commitment enforceable, creditworthy, and connected to qualified output? |
| Guarantee or credit enhancement | Protects a beneficiary from defined non-performance or credit loss | Usually no, until a claim | Guaranteed obligation or specified shortfall | Guarantor | Does the guarantor have capacity, and what behavior is it guaranteeing? |
| Insurance | Indemnifies or pays upon an insured loss or defined event | No, except claim payment | Actual loss or agreed event/amount | Licensed insurer or risk pool | Is the event insurable, fortuitous, measurable, and within policy terms? |
| Derivative or hedge | Transfers market or event risk by reference to an underlying | Usually little or none beyond collateral or premium | Price, rate, index, quantity, or event formula | Hedge counterparty or market participant | Does the payoff offset a real exposure after basis, collateral, and counterparty risk? |
| Security | Creates a transferable or regulated investment claim | Yes when issued | Equity, debt, asset cash flow, or contractual payoff | Security holder | What claim is being sold, with what disclosure, priority, and investor protections? |
| Grant, prize, or explicit subsidy | Pays for a public or philanthropic objective without a market-return claim | Yes or conditionally | Eligible cost, milestone, output, or public benefit | Grant maker or public body | Is support authorized, measurable, additional, capped, and transparently reported? |

“Risk-sharing instrument” is an umbrella description, not a legal category.
Likewise, “blended finance” describes the combination of commercial and
concessionary capital; it does not remove the need to value and disclose the
concession.

## 1. Capital formation

Capital-formation instruments supply funding and expose the provider to the
project's performance.

### Equity

Common or preferred equity absorbs residual loss and receives residual upside.
It is normally the appropriate first-loss capital for scientific,
commissioning, and market uncertainty that cannot yet support fixed payment.
Preferred terms can include liquidation preference, conversion, governance,
anti-dilution protection, and milestone-based funding.

Equity does not eliminate risk; it locates risk with an owner. Excessive
liquidation preferences, mandatory redemption, or fixed returns can make an
instrument economically debt-like and weaken later financeability.

### Debt

Loans, notes, and bonds promise interest and principal according to agreed
terms. Relevant forms may include:

- development or bridge facilities;
- construction loans with conditions precedent to each draw;
- delayed-draw or milestone facilities;
- equipment loans;
- working-capital, receivables, or inventory facilities;
- amortizing term loans; and
- subordinated or mezzanine debt.

Debt is suitable only to the extent that cash flow, collateral, sponsor
support, or another credible repayment source protects scheduled debt service.
Capitalizing interest or deferring maturity can address timing; it does not cure
negative economics.

### Leasing and vendor finance

An equipment lease or vendor facility can align payment with asset use and may
place residual-value or performance exposure with a specialist. Its value
depends on enforceable acceptance tests, maintenance obligations, removal
rights, alternative uses, and the vendor's capacity. A nominal lease may still
be debt for accounting, tax, insolvency, or regulatory purposes.

### Revenue or royalty participation

A provider may fund the project in return for a percentage of revenue, units
sold, or another cash-flow measure. This can flex with performance, but it can
also burden operating cash before the project reaches profitability. The base,
duration, cap, audit rights, product substitutions, and change-of-control terms
must be explicit.

## 2. Project finance

Project finance is a financing architecture, not one instrument. A special-
purpose project company owns or contracts for the asset, and creditors rely
principally on project cash flows and security rather than unrestricted sponsor
recourse.

A credible structure commonly requires:

- committed sponsor equity funded before or alongside senior debt;
- fixed or controlled construction scope and an independently reviewed budget;
- completion tests and support for cost overruns or delays;
- enforceable engineering, procurement, construction, technology, supply,
  operations, and offtake contracts;
- reserve accounts, cash controls, insurance, covenants, and reporting;
- a payment waterfall defining operating costs, taxes, senior debt, reserves,
  subordinated claims, and distributions;
- security interests and step-in rights that remain useful in distress; and
- a base case and downside case that support debt service without assuming
  discretionary refinancing.

First-of-a-kind cellular-agriculture facilities may not initially satisfy these
conditions. A project-finance structure becomes plausible only after completion
and operating risks are allocated to capable parties or supported by enough
loss-absorbing capital. Calling a loan “project finance” does not make it
non-recourse or bankable.

## 3. Securities and pooled vehicles

A security is a legal investment claim; it is not a separate source of economic
value. Equity shares, notes, project bonds, asset-backed securities, and fund
interests can finance projects if investors receive adequate disclosure and the
underlying cash flows support the claim.

### Project bonds and private placements

These can extend tenor and broaden the investor base after construction or
operating risk becomes acceptable. They require suitable disclosure, covenant
packages, servicing, transfer rules, and often ratings or private credit
analysis.

### Warehouse and securitization structures

A warehouse can aggregate eligible loans or project interests before longer-
term financing. A securitization can issue claims with different payment
priorities against a pool.

Tranching redistributes loss; it does not remove it. A senior tranche is
protected only by real subordination, reserves, excess spread, guarantees, or
other credit enhancement. Pool size is not evidence of diversification. Shared
cell lines, process technology, equipment, input suppliers, customers,
regulation, or public sentiment can create common-mode failure.

No pooled structure should proceed until:

- asset eligibility and exclusion rules are objective;
- asset data are comparable and independently verified;
- default, loss, recovery, and correlation assumptions have an evidence basis;
- originators retain appropriate exposure and cannot weaken underwriting;
- cash-flow waterfalls and triggers are reproducible;
- servicing and workout capacity exist; and
- investor materials disclose concentration, model uncertainty, and subsidy.

Portfolio finance is therefore a later-stage option, not a founding assumption
of this project.

## 4. Demand-support instruments

Demand support reduces revenue uncertainty or coordinates buyers and
producers. It is often more direct than transferring the same uncertainty to a
financial counterparty.

### Offtake agreement

The buyer agrees to purchase defined product under stated volume, price,
quality, delivery, and term provisions. A financeable offtake must address:

- buyer credit and any parent support;
- take-or-pay, take-and-pay, minimum-volume, and optional volume separately;
- product qualification and acceptance testing;
- price floors, ceilings, reopeners, and indexation;
- delayed commissioning, shortfall, rejection, and cure;
- termination, force majeure, change in law, and assignment; and
- lender notice, cure, and step-in rights where appropriate.

A memorandum of understanding, expression of interest, or cancellable trial is
not contracted revenue.

### Capacity reservation or availability payment

A buyer can pay for access to qualified production capacity whether or not it
uses every unit. This may fund fixed costs and provide supply security. The
contract must distinguish installed capacity, technically available capacity,
and capacity that can produce the buyer's qualified product.

### Advance purchase commitment or prepayment

A buyer, philanthropic entity, or public authority commits funding or a future
purchase subject to product and delivery requirements. Prepayment brings cash
forward and creates buyer credit exposure to the producer; escrow, refund,
security, and milestone-release terms may be necessary.

### Contract for difference or price support

One party pays the difference between an agreed strike and a defined reference
price, potentially with symmetric payments above the strike. This can bridge an
early cost gap if a credible price reference and qualified volume exist. If a
public entity pays the difference, the expected and maximum subsidy must be
reported as public support, not represented as private market demand.

### Public procurement and prizes

Procurement can create actual demand for compliant products; a prize rewards a
defined achievement. Both may support public objectives without creating a
tradable claim. Product safety, neutrality rules, competitive process, and
value-for-money remain essential.

## 5. Guarantees and credit enhancement

A guarantee is a promise to perform or pay if another party fails to meet a
defined obligation. Common forms include:

- **completion guarantee:** sponsor or contractor supports cost and performance
  until objective completion tests are met;
- **payment guarantee:** guarantor supports a buyer's, project company's, or
  borrower's payment obligation;
- **credit guarantee:** guarantor covers a stated share of lender loss;
- **performance guarantee:** vendor, contractor, or operator supports specified
  output, availability, efficiency, or quality performance;
- **first-loss support:** a defined junior amount absorbs losses before senior
  capital; and
- **political or policy-risk guarantee:** covers narrowly defined government or
  policy actions where offered by an authorized provider.

Every guarantee must state the guaranteed obligation, cap, duration, claim
procedure, exclusions, defenses, cure rights, recoveries, subrogation, and
termination. The guarantor's credit quality and liquidity under stress matter
more than the label.

A public guarantee contains a contingent public liability. Report expected
loss, stress loss, maximum exposure, fees, collateral, recoveries, and budgetary
treatment. A guarantee priced below expected cost is a subsidy and must be
identified as such.

Guarantees should not protect investors from risks retained by workers,
consumers, animals, or communities, nor should they insulate sponsors from
misconduct, fraud, avoidable safety failure, or undisclosed related-party
transactions.

## 6. Insurance

Insurance generally transfers the financial consequence of a fortuitous loss
to an insurer in exchange for premium. Potentially relevant established lines
include construction all-risk, delay in start-up, property damage, machinery
breakdown, business interruption, cargo, cyber, product liability, recall,
directors and officers, and environmental liability, subject to availability
and policy terms.

Two broad forms should be distinguished:

- **indemnity cover** pays eligible actual loss up to policy limits; and
- **parametric cover** pays an agreed amount when an objective trigger occurs,
  whether or not that amount equals actual loss.

Parametric simplicity can reduce loss-adjustment delay, but it creates basis
risk: the project may suffer without a payment, or receive a payment without an
equivalent loss. A trigger controlled by the insured creates moral hazard.

Technical underperformance that is expected, gradual, poorly measured, or
within the operator's control may be uninsurable. Insurance is not a substitute
for maintenance, quality systems, prudent reserves, or sponsor capital.

Insurance and derivatives can have similar event-based cash flows, but their
legal tests, licensing, insurable-interest requirements, indemnity principles,
capital treatment, and insolvency regimes can differ. Classification must be
determined from law and contract terms, not chosen for convenience.

## 7. Derivatives and hedges

A derivative derives value from an underlying price, rate, index, quantity, or
event. It usually reallocates an exposure rather than financing the full asset,
although an upfront premium, collateral terms, or embedded derivative can
affect liquidity.

### Conventional market-risk hedges

- Fixed-for-floating electricity or gas swaps, where the facility has a
  matching consumption exposure.
- Foreign-exchange forwards or options for imported equipment and inputs.
- Interest-rate swaps, caps, or collars for floating-rate debt.
- Commodity forwards, swaps, or options for inputs with a sufficiently close
  reference market.

The hedge quantity and tenor should follow the underlying exposure. Over-
hedging can create a speculative position if production falls or the project is
delayed. Collateral and termination payments can create liquidity demands at
the worst time even when the hedge is economically effective.

### Project-specific contingent contracts

Research candidates may include:

- a cash payment upon a rigorously defined commissioning delay;
- an availability or qualified-output shortfall swap;
- a collar on a verified input-cost basket;
- an option on qualified capacity for a committed buyer; or
- a milestone-linked contingent funding commitment.

These labels do not establish that the arrangement is a derivative. A
performance warranty, liquidated-damages clause, insurance product, option,
loan commitment, or guarantee may be the more accurate and efficient form.

### Underlying and settlement standard

Before modeling any derivative-like contract, define:

1. **Economic exposure:** the cash-flow risk the contract is intended to
   offset.
2. **Reference variable:** exact unit, quality, location, period, and source.
3. **Observation:** timestamp, data hierarchy, missing-data rule, and revision
   policy.
4. **Verification:** independent calculation agent, audit access, and dispute
   process.
5. **Settlement:** formula, cap, floor, netting, timing, currency, and tax.
6. **Volume:** fixed, forecast, or actual exposure, including over-hedge rules.
7. **Disruption:** contamination, outage, redesign, force majeure, regulatory
   change, cessation of reference, and fallback.
8. **Credit support:** collateral, margin, guarantee, close-out, and recovery.
9. **Basis risk:** conditions in which project loss and contract payment differ.
10. **Conduct controls:** position limits, access, reporting, conflicts, and
    anti-manipulation provisions.

No reference based solely on operator-reported nameplate capacity, unaudited
cost, or discretionary product acceptance is suitable for external settlement.

## Choosing the least complex effective instrument

Use the following sequence:

1. Remove or operationally mitigate the risk where economical.
2. Allocate controllable risk by ordinary construction, supply, operating, or
   sale contract.
3. Retain risk with appropriately sized equity and reserves where no capable
   external bearer exists.
4. Use established insurance or a guarantee for a defined loss or obligation.
5. Use a conventional hedge where a liquid and sufficiently matching reference
   exists.
6. Consider a bespoke derivative only when the exposure, trigger, counterparty,
   collateral, and regulatory treatment are all clearer or more efficient than
   the alternatives.
7. Consider pooling or transferable securities only after asset comparability,
   performance history, servicing, and correlation analysis support them.

Complexity must earn its cost by improving at least one measurable outcome:
capital availability, cost of capital, tenor, cash-flow stability, completion
probability, or resilience. Regulatory arbitrage, accounting appearance, and
fee generation are not valid project outcomes.

## Candidate-instrument scorecard

Every candidate should be compared with an unstructured baseline and the
simplest conventional alternative.

| Test | Required disclosure |
| --- | --- |
| Diagnosed failure | The exact financing or risk-allocation problem addressed |
| Incremental funding | Amount and timing of capital that becomes available because of the instrument |
| Economic additionality | Effect on capacity, completion date, tenor, or financing cost relative to the counterfactual |
| Risk transfer | Exposure before and after the contract for every material party |
| Expected value | Expected payments, fees, premiums, losses, recoveries, and returns |
| Tail behavior | Severe-but-plausible losses, liquidity calls, termination amounts, and wrong-way risk |
| Basis and model risk | Mismatch between reference and loss, parameter basis, and sensitivity to assumptions |
| Incentives | Ability of any party to influence the trigger, reporting, safety, or production decision |
| Subsidy | Provider, legal authority, expected value, maximum exposure, and beneficiaries |
| Legal character | Preliminary classification and all required specialist reviews |
| Operational burden | Data, verification, collateral, accounting, reporting, and dispute cost |
| Mission effect | Conservative estimate of incremental qualified capacity and animal-welfare contribution |
| Exit and failure | Cure, termination, workout, restructuring, and decommissioning process |

An instrument fails the scorecard if its claimed benefit disappears after fees,
collateral, expected losses, subsidy, or operational burden are included.

## Market-integrity boundaries

Until a reference market is independently administered, sufficiently robust,
and supported by real commercial exposure, project-specific instruments should
be bilateral or narrowly placed with sophisticated, capable counterparties.

The project will not endorse:

- sale to parties who cannot understand or bear the loss;
- net speculative notional materially above documented commercial exposure;
- leverage that depends on continuous refinancing or collateral liquidity;
- a benchmark controlled by a payment beneficiary;
- artificial trading intended to manufacture a price history;
- a pooled product whose diversification claim ignores common-mode risk;
- a structure designed chiefly to avoid securities, derivatives, insurance,
  banking, consumer-protection, or public-budget rules;
- a guarantee or concession whose public cost is omitted from returns; or
- transaction terms that reward compromised food safety, worker safety,
  animal welfare, environmental compliance, or truthful reporting.

## Initial research order

The first structures to test should be ordinary and exposure-led:

1. milestone-gated development and construction capital;
2. qualified offtake or capacity reservation with a creditworthy buyer;
3. vendor performance support and equipment finance;
4. explicit first-loss or completion support with a disclosed cap;
5. conventional energy, foreign-exchange, and interest-rate hedges; and
6. only then, a bespoke contract for a residual, measurable commissioning or
   operating exposure.

This order is a research priority, not a conclusion that every facility needs
all six mechanisms.
